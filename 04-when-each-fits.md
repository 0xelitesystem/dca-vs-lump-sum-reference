# When each fits

Lump sum fits when you can tolerate an immediate loss and want the highest average exposure to market growth. Dollar-cost averaging fits when a sharp drop right after investing would rattle you, or when you are investing income as it arrives rather than a sum you already hold.

## When lump sum fits

Lump sum fits when you have a sum ready, a long time horizon, and the temperament to hold through an early loss without selling. The longer your horizon, the more the average upward drift works in favor of being fully invested sooner. If a drop the week after you invest would not change your behavior, the average case favors lump sum.

## When DCA fits

Dollar-cost averaging fits when the worst-case immediate loss matters to you more than the average return, when you are nervous about investing at what might be a high point, or when the money is large relative to your total assets so a bad entry would hurt. It also fits when avoiding regret is what keeps you in the market at all.

## The case where DCA is automatic

There is a common case where DCA is not even a choice: investing income as it arrives. If you invest part of each paycheck, you are dollar-cost averaging by default, because you do not have the full sum up front to invest at once. This is the most natural form of DCA and it sidesteps the timing debate entirely, since there is no lump to deploy.

## Avoiding the false comparison

The DCA versus lump sum debate only applies when you actually hold a lump sum and could invest it all now. If you do not have a lump, investing steadily as money comes in is simply how investing works, not a strategy you chose over lump sum. Keeping that distinction clear prevents a lot of confused comparison.
