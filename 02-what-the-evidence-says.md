# What the evidence says

Across long market histories, investing a lump sum has on average ended ahead of dollar-cost averaging the same amount, because markets rise more often than they fall. Studies of historical returns tend to find lump sum beats DCA a majority of the time, though not always, and the gap is not enormous.

## Why lump sum tends to win

Markets spend more time rising than falling over long periods, and they have historically trended upward. Money invested earlier is therefore exposed to more of that upward drift. Dollar-cost averaging holds part of the money in cash while it waits to be invested, and cash on the sidelines misses the average upward movement. The longer money sits uninvested, the more expected growth it forgoes.

## The majority, not the certainty

Lump sum winning on average and most of the time does not mean it always wins. In the minority of cases where the market falls soon after investing, DCA comes out ahead, because it had not yet committed the full amount. The average favors lump sum, but the distribution includes outcomes where DCA was the better timing choice. Average results describe the typical case, not your specific one.

## The size of the gap

The historical advantage of lump sum is real but usually modest, not dramatic. This matters because a modest expected-return advantage can be outweighed by other factors, such as how you would react to an immediate loss. A small edge on average is easy to give up in exchange for a smoother ride if the smoother ride keeps you invested.

## What the evidence does not tell you

History describes what has tended to happen, not what will happen next, and not how you will feel watching it. The evidence can inform the decision but cannot make it, because the right choice depends on your risk tolerance and your reaction to loss, which no historical average captures.
